## Start with the guest, not the recipe

A profitable cocktail menu begins with your guest profile. A luxury resort audience may want complex, story-driven signatures. A beach club with high throughput needs fast, batchable, photo-ready serves. A restaurant bar should complement the food menu and turn tables efficiently.

Define price bands early. Your cheapest cocktail should attract trial; your mid-range should carry margin; your premium serve should create aspiration and social sharing.

## Engineer for margin first

Every drink on the menu should be costed to a target pour cost, typically 18–24%. Build a costing card for each recipe with exact millilitre measures, garnish, ice and glassware cost. If a drink cannot hit the target without an unacceptable price, redesign it or drop it.

Contribution margin matters more than pour cost alone. A cocktail with a slightly higher pour cost but a much higher contribution can be more valuable than a lower-pour-cost drink with a small contribution.

## Structure the list

Limit the menu to 8–12 cocktails. Too many choices slow decisions and increase waste. Group drinks by style: light and refreshing, bold and spirit-forward, tropical and local, low or no alcohol. Include at least one low-ABV and one zero-proof option.

Use names and descriptions that tell a story. In Bali, local botanicals, arak, tropical fruit and Balinese place names give guests something to remember and share.

## Write spec cards and train the team

A spec card should include the recipe, method, glassware, ice spec, garnish, batch instructions, sell price and target pour cost. Photo-illustrated cards reduce variance between bartenders and protect margin during busy shifts.

Every bartender should be able to make every drink blind to spec before launch. Schedule a training session and tasting panel, and capture feedback before the menu goes live.

## Plan for seasonality and refresh

Seasonal ingredients in Bali shift with the fruit calendar and tourist seasons. Build a refresh calendar: minor tweaks quarterly, full menu review twice a year. Track sales mix and remove underperformers rather than letting them occupy menu real estate.

A menu that is never refreshed feels tired. A menu that changes too often confuses regulars and breaks training. Aim for a rhythm that keeps the list familiar but gives guests a reason to return.

## Context: Why cocktail menu design is profit engineering

A cocktail menu is more than a list of drinks. It is a sales tool, a margin plan and a brand statement all at once. In Bali, where guests have endless choices and social media amplifies every weak serve, a well-engineered menu can become a genuine competitive advantage. The goal is to create drinks that match your concept, excite your guests and hit a pour-cost target that protects your margin.

This guide covers the full process: starting with the guest profile, engineering for margin, structuring the list, writing spec cards and planning for seasonality. Whether you are opening a new venue or refreshing an existing list, these principles will help you build a cocktail menu that sells.

A common trap is to treat the menu as a creative exercise alone. Creativity matters, but it must sit inside a commercial frame. A beautiful drink that takes three minutes to make, costs 35% in ingredients and confuses the guest is a liability, not an asset. The best menus balance originality with executability and margin.

## Common mistakes: Common cocktail menu mistakes in Bali

- Designing the menu around bartender favourites rather than guest profile and price band.
- Listing too many cocktails, which slows service, increases waste and confuses guests.
- Failing to cost recipes to the millilitre, leading to unknown pour cost and drifting margin.
- Launching without training every bartender to make every drink blind to spec.
- Letting the menu go stale; a static list feels tired and misses seasonal ingredient opportunities.

## Actionable tips: Actionable tips for a profitable cocktail menu

- Define guest profile, price bands and target pour cost before writing a single recipe.
- Keep the list to 8–12 cocktails with clear style groups and at least one low or no-ABV option.
- Build costing cards with exact measures and update them when supplier prices change.
- Create photo-illustrated spec cards and train the team to execute blind to spec before launch.
- Schedule minor menu tweaks quarterly and a full review twice a year based on sales mix.
